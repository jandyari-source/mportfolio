About me

Operations is where a business wins or loses.

I'm a junior from New Carrollton, Maryland, studying Industrial & Operations Engineering in the Honors Engineering Program. My path to IOE started with a vintage clothing business I ran on Depop. Shipping delays frustrated my customers, and I was spending more on sourcing clothes than I made selling them. That showed me that operations is where a business wins or loses. Through NSBE and IISE, I found a field built around fixing exactly those problems.

Today I'm VP of Recruitment for the Michigan Engineering Consulting Group, a business analyst with Michigan Innovation Ventures, and an MLT Consulting Fellow. I'm a native English and Spanish speaker, and above all I value connection: my best work comes from teams that genuinely enjoy working together. My goal is to become a leader in consulting, solving hard operational problems while building teams people want to be part of.

Leadership How I developed it

The situation: When I became VP of Recruitment in January 2025, there was no shared rubric, so two reviewers could read the same application very differently. I wanted every candidate to get the same fair shot.

What I did: I introduced a standardized evaluation framework so every board member scored candidates on the same criteria, and I coordinated 5+ recruitment events that reached 300+ students. Applications grew from 113 to 200 (+77%), and we selected the top 10%.

How it developed my leadership: I learned that leading isn't about making every call myself. It's about building a standard a whole board believes in and can use without me in the room. My DISC results (Influence, Counselor pattern) showed that I naturally lead by bringing people together; recruitment taught me to pair that with structure.

Entrepreneurial Mindset How I developed it

The situation: In Spring 2026, Legendary Sports Xperiences asked MECG to help launch Go Play Flag at the Big House, a brand-new youth flag football tournament at Michigan Stadium. There was no past event to copy.

What I did: As project manager for six analysts, I turned that open-ended ask into workstreams each analyst owned: scheduling and brackets, field operations, fan experience, marketing, and volunteers. We researched competing youth leagues, compared ways to reach families such as parent-focused social media ads, school and PTO partnerships, and a coach ambassador program, and built reusable checklists and staffing plans.

How it developed my entrepreneurial mindset: I learned to act on an ambiguous problem by defining it first, comparing options, and building something the client can reuse in future seasons. It also tested a weakness my DISC profile named: taking on too much. Leading recruitment and a client project at the same time taught me to give teammates real ownership instead of doing everything myself.

What I learned: Good leadership means building systems a team can run without you.

At MIV, student analysts work on projects with venture capital firms and startups. I've worked on two separate engagements.

Risk Management How I developed it Renaissance Venture Capital · Winter 2026

The situation: Early-stage investors have to decide which startups to back with very little information, and Renaissance Venture Capital wanted a more consistent way to screen them.

What I did: I helped build a 100-point AI-powered framework that scores startups on founder-market fit, market size, go-to-market strategy, business model, and traction. Rand* Construction · Washington, DC · Summer 2026

Making a $30M project visible

Project Operations Intern

  • Systems Thinking
  • Ethics

Systems Thinking How I developed it

The situation: A construction project is a system of budgets, vendors, schedules, and clients, and a delay in one place shows up as cost somewhere else.

What I did: For Freshfields' $30M headquarters renovation, I built Power BI and Excel reporting that connected $23M+ in committed costs, $6.9M+ in projected expenditures, procurement performance, and $2.9M+ in forecast-to-complete, then turned it into weekly progress decks the client used to make decisions. For Indiana University's D.C. campus redevelopment, I analyzed budgets, procurement trends, and forecasts, supporting a 43% reduction in projected costs ($14M to $8M).

How it developed my systems thinking: I learned to trace one number back through the whole system, from a purchase to a schedule change to the final budget, while keeping the client's big-picture goal in view.

Ethics How I developed it

The situation: Clients commit to sustainability goals like LEED certification, and those commitments only hold if every purchase actually meets them.

What I did: I evaluated 300+ material submittals and $3M+ in environmentally preferred procurement against LEED requirements, and built dashboards that reduced compliance risk and improved LEED alignment by 71%.

How it developed my ethics: Ethics in engineering isn't only about avoiding obvious wrongdoing. It's integrity in the details: holding every material to the commitment the client made, so the finished building is what it claims to be.

What I learned: Data only matters when it's clear enough for an executive to act on.

Class projects

IOE 461: Six Sigma Case Study

Quality Engineering · Fall 2026

  • Risk Management
  • Systems Thinking

My 4-person team used DMAIC on an accounts payable department losing about $470K a year to late payments, rework, and missed discounts. Using 8 months of records and 6,954 invoices in Minitab, we found the cause was the process, not staffing: a first-in, first-out queue missed 62.8% of early-payment discounts. Our recommendations projected on-time payment rising from 75.6% to 98% and about $376K saved per year.

How it developed my risk management: We didn't stop at the savings. We calculated a $90K worst case if waived mismatches were all overcharges, then designed a monthly audit and a two-region pilot to control it. I learned that a strong recommendation names its own risks.

IOE 373: Bank Loan Campaign

Data Analytics · Winter 2026

  • Ethics
  • Communication

My team built a logistic regression model on 5,000 customers to predict who would accept a personal-loan offer, after a campaign with about a 9% acceptance rate. It reached 95.8% accuracy on held-out data, and we turned the top predictors (income, education, CD accounts, family size) into a targeting plan.

How it developed my ethics: Looking back, the model missed about a third of likely acceptors, and inputs like ZIP code could steer loan offers unfairly. It taught me that accurate isn't the same as fair, and that I need to check models for both.

ENGR 100: Curing the Healthcare System

Operations Engineer · Feb–Apr 2025

  • Teamwork
  • Empathy

I led a 4-person team building a Python (SimPy) simulation of Michigan Medicine's Sports Clinic scheduling. A 56-slot schedule raised on-time access from 87.3% to 94.2% and cut projected cost from $350K to about $277K, which we presented to stakeholders as a consulting-style poster.

How it developed my teamwork and empathy: Leading my first engineering team meant splitting the simulation, cost analysis, and poster so each person owned a piece. Keeping patients at the center pushed us to protect access, not just cut cost, because behind every percentage is someone waiting for care.
